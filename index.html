Following the money · Australia

Is Australia a Big Spender?

Trillions of dollars flow through Australia's public finances over time. That money keeps hospitals running, funds schools, supports people through retirement and helps build the infrastructure we rely on. But how big is Australia's spending bill, who gets the biggest share, and what happens when the country's priorities collide with its budget? Follow the money to find out.

The gap is getting tighter.

The dramatic part of this story came in 2020-21. The government had to respond to the pandemic, from economic support to business subsidies. Was the pandemic an extraordinary spending sock, or did it permanently change the scale of government spending. In 2023-24, governments funded about 69.6% of the total health expenditure with the rest coming from individuals and private health insurers.

How does Australia compare?

A billion dollars means something different in a country of five million people than in one of thirty million. Comparing Australia's spending with international peers helps separate the size of the country from the scale of government

HERE'S THE CATCH!!

With international rankings: a country can spend more in total simply because it has more people, while a smaller country can spend more per person. These are two different ways of measuring the same public sector and they can produce very different rankings. So before declaring Australia a big spender, ask what the chart is actually measuring.

Chapter 3 · Spending Breakdown

Health is a major spending commitment

Roughly 4.6 million Australians are 65+ years old, this is projected to continue growing rapidly. The government provides heavily subsidised resources and care for older people.

The Scale of Australia's Health Spending

Source: ABS, Government Finance Statistics, Annual 2024-25.

The pandemic spending surge has not simply vanished.

During COVID-19, governments spent heavily on emergency responses, testing, vaccines and other public health measures. As those extraordinary programs wound down, the underlying demand for hospitals, medical appointments and community care remained. The emergency changed, but the long-term pressure on the health system did not disappear.

Do more hospitals mean more money?

Hospital infrastructure is geographically uneven.

Australia's population is concentrated along the coast and around major cities, while enormous inland regions have far fewer people spread across much larger distances. A nearby hospital and an accessible hospital are not always the same thing.

Tasmania faces a different kind of health challenge.

Tasmania has Australia's oldest population by median age among the states, increasing the importance of aged care, chronic disease management and services that help people remain independent. Can a smaller population really cost more to care for?

The Northern Territory has the highest per-person spending.

With a very sparse population, it costs a lot to deliver medical care in the Northern Territory. Health costs are forced to include transport to reach patients.

Where does all the health expenditure go?

Health spending covers a range of services, from hospitals and outpatient care to community and public health services. Some costs are highly visible, such as hospital buildings and emergency departments. Others are less obvious such as subsidised medicines, patient transport and preventive programs that aim to stop illness before it becomes a crisis.

Does spending more always mean getting better care?

According to comprehensive global rankings by The Commonwealth Fund (which tracks the healthcare performance of high-income nations), Australia's healthcare system consistently places in the top 3 overall globally. While the United States spends a staggering 16.5% of its GDP on healthcare to rank last among peer nations, Australia spends only about 10.5% of its GDP to achieve significantly higher life expectancy, lower infant mortality, and superior disease recovery rates.
